📝 Overview / What is Alzheimer's?

Alzheimer's disease is a type of dementia that affects memory, thinking ability and overall behavior. It is a serious condition that could cause significant interference to one's daily life. Alzheimer's disease accounts for 60-80% of dementia cases.

🔬 Key Research Finding:

Alzheimer's is not considered a normal part of aging. The greatest known risk factor is increasing age, and the majority of people with Alzheimer's are 65 and older. Alzheimer's disease is considered to be younger-onset Alzheimer's if it affects a person under 65. Younger-onset Alzheimer's can also be reffered to as "Early-onset Alzheimer's". People with younger-onset Alzheimer's can be in the early, middle or late stage of the disease.

🧠 The Brain & Alzheimer's

The brain of a person with Alzheimer's looks comparatively shrunken or damaged, kind of like a walnut. Returning to the subject, a human brain has 100 billion nerve cells called neurons. Each nerve cell group together to form networks to carry out basic life functioning. To do their work, brain cells act like tiny factories. They help generating energy, receiving supplies, exchange and store information with other cells. Scientists believe Alzheimer's disease prevents a part in the cell factory (just like a real one) to carry out its work, therefore causing breakdowns and problems. Moreover, Alzheimer's disease is caused by the abnormal buildup of proteins in the brain, specifically amyloid plaques that form around brain cells and tau tangles that develop inside them. This accumulation damages and ultimately destroys neurons, leading to shrinkage of brain tissue and the cognitive decline associated with dementia.

💔 Impact on Others

Alzheimer's disease heavily disrupts family dynamics, often termed a "family disease." The impact radiates outward, fundamentally altering roles, routines and relationships while taking a severe toll on the emotional, financial and physical well-being of caregivers and loved ones.

📖 Caregiver Impact:

In context of alzheimer's disease, caregiver impact refers to the profound and multifaceted toll on a individual-typically a spouse or adult child who provides daily care. It wears them down in many ways: physically (no sleep, back problems, neglecting their own health), emotionally (sadness, guilt, frustration, loneliness), socially (no time with friends or hobbies), and financially (losing a job or spending their savings). In short, the caregiver suffers as much as a person with Alzheimer's (most cases), just in different ways.p>

💊 Treatment & Care

While there is no cure, the "best" treatment depends on the stage of the disease and is typically a combination of disease-modifying therapies, symptom-managing medications, and lifestyle interventions to slow decline and maximize quality of life. Some of them are listed below and explained in a simpler way: 1. Disease-Modifying Immunotherapies: These treatments target the underlying cause of Alzheimer's by clearing beta-amyloid plaques from the brain. They are most effective in the early stages. 2. Symptom-Managing Medications: Medications such as Cholinesterase Inhibitors, Glutamate Inhibitors and combination drugs boost brain chemicals to help manage memory, thinking, and psychiatric symptoms.

3. Non-Drug Interventions & Lifestyle: Maintaing a healthy diet along with regular physical exercise, taking part in cognitive and social engagement and involve in behavioral management (non-invasive therapies and counselling) help manage the disease.

📚 My Research Notes

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive brain disorder and the most common cause of dementia, primarily affecting older adults over 65. It slowly destroys memory, thinking skills, and eventually the ability to perform basic daily tasks. The disease is caused by abnormal protein buildup in the brain (plaques and tangles) that damages and kills brain cells. Early symptoms include forgetting recent conversations, getting lost in familiar places, and personality changes. As it progresses, individuals may not recognize loved ones, have difficulty speaking or swallowing, and require full-time care. There is currently no cure, only treatments that temporarily slow symptoms.
